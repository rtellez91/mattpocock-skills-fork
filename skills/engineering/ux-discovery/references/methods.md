# Selecting a method

Use this as a decision aid, not a checklist to execute in order. Begin with available product evidence. Each method must answer a named uncertainty that matters to the current scope.

| Question | Candidate method | Evidence or useful output | Limit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What problem occurs, for whom, and under what conditions? | Review support, analytics, existing research; interviews or observation when needed | Grounded needs, context, constraints, frequency where data supports it | Stakeholder recollection is not automatically representative of users |
| What happens before, during, and after the task across channels? | Journey mapping | Actions, touchpoints, interruptions, and evidence-backed friction | Mark inferred needs and emotions as hypotheses |
| Which people, systems, and internal processes must coordinate? | Service blueprint | Customer-facing steps, backstage responsibilities, handoffs, and failure points | Use only the scope needed for the decision, not a complete organizational map |
| What actions, choices, prerequisites, and recovery paths should exist? | Task flow and concrete scenarios | A coherent sequence with relevant alternatives and state transitions | A plausible diagram does not demonstrate that people understand it |
| How should information be grouped, named, and found? | Information architecture exploration; card sorting or tree testing when user evidence is needed | Candidate structures and evidence about categorization or findability | Agent-generated groupings remain hypotheses |
| Which hierarchy or interaction approach fits the task? | Wireframes, visual alternatives, or focused prototypes | Comparable approaches and a reasoned selection | Hold requirements and useful product foundations constant while comparing alternatives |
| Can people understand and complete the task? | Usability testing with representative participants and realistic tasks | Observed completion, confusion, errors, and recovery | Expert inspection or an agent simulation is not a participant test |
| Does an existing experience have identifiable usability or accessibility problems? | Evidence-based audit or expert review | Findings tied to captured states and relevant criteria | Separate inspection findings from measured user outcomes and runtime accessibility evidence |

Journey maps and blueprints synthesize evidence; producing the artifact does not create that evidence. If participants, analytics, or source material are unavailable, identify what can be inspected now and what remains hypothetical. Continue only the work that does not depend on the missing evidence.

## Fidelity and stopping

Specify the dimensions relevant to the question:

- **Content:** representative labels, realistic values, density, lengths, and consequential messages.
- **Interaction:** the actions and state transitions needed to examine the task, with simulated data unless actual integration is explicitly in scope.
- **Appearance:** enough hierarchy, sizing, and visual detail to examine the question. Reuse accepted design foundations; reserve new art direction for a real visual decision.

For a responsibility question, a scenario or map may be sufficient. For a navigation question, content and transitions may matter more than finish. For visual direction, high visual fidelity may be useful even without interactivity. An image can illustrate an interaction idea but cannot prove that interaction works.

State the decision criterion before making the artifact. Stop when it is met. Increase fidelity or change method only when the remaining question justifies it. Escalate unresolved product tradeoffs to the human; do not manufacture certainty by producing more variants.
